# Summary

This note expands selected prescription-level rows into counselor-style narratives. Each case is written the way a SHIP-style counselor or Medicare analyst might explain the decision to a beneficiary or caregiver.

> [!info]
> These narratives are illustrative training cases built from the vault's policy and evidence notes. They are designed to show time-based cost reasoning, not to substitute for a real Plan Finder run on a live beneficiary record.

## Case 1: Low Utilizer On Rosuvastatin During Annual Enrollment

### Beneficiary Background

- Beneficiary age or eligibility pathway: `67`, standard age-in enrollee now re-shopping during annual enrollment
- Trigger event: annual enrollment
- Current coverage: standalone PDP with a low advertised premium
- Pharmacy preference: local preferred retail chain
- Current affordability concern: the beneficiary wants to know whether the lowest premium plan is still the best choice

### Prescription List

- `Rosuvastatin` monthly fill

### Month-By-Month Cost Logic

- `January`: the beneficiary may face early deductible exposure on the first fill, depending on the plan's deductible design.
- `February`: if deductible remains, part of the second fill can still be affected.
- `March` through `December`: once the early deductible exposure is absorbed, the pattern becomes stable and predictable because the regimen is simple and low cost.
- In this kind of case, the annual OOP cap usually never becomes active. The main financial question is not catastrophic protection but whether premium plus routine pharmacy cost is lower in one safe plan than another.

### Recommendation

> [!success]
> Recommended counseling action: compare the full annual cost at the actual preferred pharmacy and do not let the beneficiary choose by premium alone.

Why:
- The vault's plan-comparison notes repeatedly emphasize `annual cost, not premium only`.
- A low-premium plan can still lose if it has a worse pharmacy price or a less favorable deductible pattern for the beneficiary's real fill schedule.

Risk or caveat:
- This looks like a simple case, but it can still go wrong if the plan's preferred-pharmacy terms changed or if a second drug is added mid-year.

Follow-up needed:
- Confirm whether the beneficiary expects any new prescriptions in the coming year.

## Case 2: Insulin User On Levemir With Possible LIS Eligibility

### Beneficiary Background

- Beneficiary age or eligibility pathway: `71`, retired beneficiary with diabetes and limited income
- Trigger event: annual enrollment plus possible subsidy review
- Current coverage: PDP with stable enrollment from the prior year
- Pharmacy preference: neighborhood pharmacy used for all chronic medications
- Current affordability concern: monthly insulin costs feel manageable, but total pharmacy spending still creates cash-flow stress

### Prescription List

- `Levemir` monthly fill
- `Rosuvastatin` monthly fill

### Month-By-Month Cost Logic

- `January`: insulin protections reduce the usual deductible shock for `Levemir`, but the non-insulin drug can still create deductible exposure.
- `February` through `April`: insulin remains relatively predictable, while the companion drug continues to shape the beneficiary's real out-of-pocket pattern.
- `May` through `December`: if the regimen stays stable, the case becomes a question of steady monthly affordability rather than cap-hit timing.
- The key branch in this case is LIS. If the beneficiary qualifies for Extra Help, the entire annual liability changes because each fill is recalculated under subsidy rules rather than standard non-LIS cost sharing.

### Recommendation

> [!success]
> Recommended counseling action: screen for LIS before recommending payment smoothing or before concluding that the current plan is unaffordable.

Why:
- The vault's LIS note says subsidy status can change the beneficiary's real out-of-pocket exposure more than any single formulary rule.
- The insulin notes show why insulin users are especially sensitive to monthly affordability even after the IRA insulin protections.

Risk or caveat:
- It is easy to overfocus on the insulin cap and miss that the non-insulin part of the regimen still determines real annual affordability.

Follow-up needed:
- Check whether the plan is above or below the LIS benchmark premium and whether the beneficiary may need application help.

## Case 3: High-Cost Oncology User On Imbruvica Across Redesign Years

### Beneficiary Background

- Beneficiary age or eligibility pathway: `69`, established Medicare beneficiary with a hematologic cancer diagnosis
- Trigger event: new high-cost drug use plus annual plan review
- Current coverage: PDP with specialty-pharmacy requirements
- Pharmacy preference: specialty channel determined by plan rules
- Current affordability concern: fear of very high first-month cost and uncertainty about what changes between `CY2024` and `CY2025`

### Prescription List

- `Imbruvica` monthly fill

### Month-By-Month Cost Logic

- `January` under `CY2024`: the first fill can effectively consume the deductible and push the beneficiary to the catastrophic threshold within the first month in many plan designs.
- `February` through `December` under `CY2024`: once catastrophic coverage is reached, beneficiary cost sharing for covered drugs drops sharply in this repository's approximation.
- `January` under `CY2025`: the same type of very high-cost first fill can still dominate the year, but the logic is now organized around the redesigned annual OOP cap rather than the older gap structure.
- `After the cap is hit` under `CY2025`: covered Part D cost sharing drops to `$0` for the rest of the year.
- The practical result is that this case is dominated by access safety and year-rule differences, not by small premium differences.

### Recommendation

> [!success]
> Recommended counseling action: eliminate unsafe plans first, then compare total annual cost only among plans with realistic specialty access and acceptable utilization management.

Why:
- The vault treats high-cost oncology drugs such as `Imbruvica` as the clearest examples of why the redesign matters.
- The first month determines much of the annual cost path, so fill ordering and policy year cannot be ignored.

Risk or caveat:
- A plan can appear financially attractive but still be operationally dangerous if specialty access, prior authorization, or refill continuity is weak.

Follow-up needed:
- Verify the required specialty pharmacy, prior authorization burden, and whether the beneficiary's prescriber network can support that workflow.

## Case 4: Ozempic User Facing Coinsurance And Prior Authorization Pressure

### Beneficiary Background

- Beneficiary age or eligibility pathway: `72`, long-time Medicare enrollee with type 2 diabetes
- Trigger event: annual enrollment after a difficult year of GLP-1 cost sharing
- Current coverage: standalone PDP
- Pharmacy preference: preferred retail pharmacy, open to mail order if clearly cheaper
- Current affordability concern: the drug is covered, but the beneficiary still experiences high cost and administrative friction

### Prescription List

- `Ozempic` monthly fill

### Month-By-Month Cost Logic

- `January`: deductible exposure can still matter because `Ozempic` is not protected like Part D insulin.
- `February` through `December`: the beneficiary can continue to face elevated monthly costs if the plan uses coinsurance rather than a flat copay.
- Throughout the year, prior authorization can be as important as price because delays or renewals create non-financial access burden.
- In this case, the annual OOP cap still matters for the heaviest spenders, but the more typical counseling problem is persistent monthly pressure combined with utilization management.

### Recommendation

> [!success]
> Recommended counseling action: compare PDP and MA-PD options carefully, focusing on prior authorization, tier placement, and coinsurance pattern rather than asking only whether the drug is covered.

Why:
- The vault's GLP-1 notes say `Ozempic` use rose sharply and that plans responded in `CY2025` with nearly universal prior authorization and more coinsurance, especially in PDPs.
- This is a classic example of why formulary inclusion does not equal easy access.

Risk or caveat:
- A beneficiary may interpret "covered" as "affordable and routine," which is often false in GLP-1 cases.

Follow-up needed:
- Check whether the beneficiary uses the drug only for a diabetes indication and whether a different plan structure improves the month-to-month cost pattern.

## Case 5: Humira Versus A Humira Biosimilar In A Weak-Steering Formulary

### Beneficiary Background

- Beneficiary age or eligibility pathway: `70`, Medicare beneficiary with an autoimmune condition
- Trigger event: annual enrollment after hearing that biosimilars should lower costs
- Current coverage: PDP that lists both `Humira` and at least one Humira biosimilar
- Pharmacy preference: specialty or limited distribution channel as required
- Current affordability concern: the beneficiary expected lower cost after biosimilar entry but does not see a clear savings difference

### Prescription List

- `Humira` maintenance fills or a Humira biosimilar maintenance fill

### Month-By-Month Cost Logic

- `January`: the beneficiary starts the year expecting a cheaper alternative because a biosimilar exists.
- `February` through `December`: if the plan places `Humira` and the biosimilar on similar tiers with similar restrictions, the month-by-month beneficiary experience may remain almost the same.
- The problem is not only whether the biosimilar is technically on the formulary. The real question is whether the plan actively favors it through lower cost sharing or easier access.
- As a result, the annual pattern can stay disappointingly flat even after a new competitor enters the market.

### Recommendation

> [!success]
> Recommended counseling action: compare the reference product and biosimilar inside the same plan and pharmacy workflow, and do not assume that biosimilar presence means real beneficiary savings.

Why:
- The vault's biosimilar note says Humira biosimilar coverage improved in `CY2025`, but plans rarely used preferential placement to steer beneficiaries toward lower-cost options.
- This is one of the clearest cases where formulary design, not just drug competition, determines whether savings reach the beneficiary.

Risk or caveat:
- The beneficiary may switch plans expecting savings and then discover that the new plan is neutral between the two products.

Follow-up needed:
- Check the exact biosimilar covered, any specialty-pharmacy constraints, and whether the prescriber is willing to support a product switch if it is financially meaningful.
