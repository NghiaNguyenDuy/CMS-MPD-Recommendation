From day one, President Trump has been dedicated to lowering the cost of prescription drugs and has directed the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to take action. In 2017, we made policy changes in the Medicare Part D program that led to the lowest Part D basic premiums in 7 years. We did not stop there. CMS continued to seek out opportunities to reduce the out-of-pocket cost of insulin for patients with diabetes, which is the seventh leading cause of death in the U.S. Diabetes affects 26.8 percent of U.S. adults aged 65 years to or older and drives more than $300 billion in annual healthcare spending.

One in three Medicare beneficiaries has diabetes, with over 3.3 million Medicare beneficiaries relying on one or more of the common forms of insulin. For some of these beneficiaries, access to insulin is a critical component of their medical management. Gaps in access increase the risk of serious complications, which range from vision loss and nerve damage to chronic kidney disease, foot ulcers (which can lead to amputation if not caught), and heart attacks. Unfortunately, the price of insulin is sometimes a barrier to medical management for beneficiaries with diabetes, with studies showing patients often ration or go without.

Most Medicare beneficiaries have coverage for insulin under Medicare Part D, which is the part of the Medicare benefit that provides coverage for prescription drugs that are usually self-administered. Part D plans generally provide broad access to insulin for their enrollees, including rapid-acting, short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting insulins, as well as concentrated and pre-mixed formulations. However, based on the structure of Part D, out-of-pocket costs often vary depending on the phase of benefit the person finds themselves, leaving beneficiaries to pay a deductible and then—in the coverage gap—25 percent of the negotiated price.

CMS’s Part D Senior Savings Model is designed to lower prescription drug costs by providing Medicare patients with Part D plans that offer the broad set of insulins that beneficiaries use at a stable, affordable, and predictable cost of no more than $35 for a 30-day supply. The Model aims to increase patient access and adherence to insulin and slow the progression of the disease, which would ultimately lead to improved patient outcomes and better quality of care. While the Model provides an opportunity for Part D sponsors and pharmaceutical manufacturers to partner together in decreasing patient out-of-pocket costs, CMS is also requiring participating manufacturers to report any increases in list prices outside of the Model to protect against the possibility of program costs for insulin increasing under the Model.

CMS will consider opening the Model to other drugs in the future after we understand the impact of lowering costs for insulin.

## Barriers To Lowering Costs In Part D

The defined standard benefit design for a Part D plan is specified in law and consists of four phases—a deductible phase, an initial coverage phase, a coverage gap phase, and a catastrophic phase. (Exhibit 1) A beneficiary’s out-of-pocket costs, along with what the Part D plan pays before the coverage gap, move the beneficiary through the phases. In each phase, the beneficiary is typically responsible for paying a certain percentage of their prescription drug’s negotiated price, which is the price the Part D plan has agreed to pay to the pharmacy and closely matches the drug’s list price. The beneficiary’s Part D plan, prescription drug manufacturers, and the federal government (Medicare) pay the rest.

In addition to the defined standard benefit design, there are two other basic Part D plan designs—actuarially equivalent Part D plans and basic alternative Part D plans. These basic plans must be actuarially equivalent to the defined standard benefit design; therefore, while a basic plan can decrease a beneficiary’s out-of-pocket costs for particular drugs below the percentage specified in statute, or establish a copay instead of coinsurance structure, it typically must increase the cost-sharing amounts for other drugs to maintain actuarial equivalence.

Enhanced alternative prescription drug plans, on the other hand, are more generous than these basic benefit designs. In exchange for providing additional coverage, such as offering lower cost sharing or covering more drugs, enhanced plans charge an additional premium. In 2020, the average enhanced alternative plan Part D monthly premium is $49.32, which is approximately $17 higher than the $32.09 average basic plan premium. Therefore, enhanced plans can decrease cost sharing for particular drugs without having to increase cost sharing elsewhere, as long as the additional benefits are paid for through the supplemental premium, which is added to the base beneficiary premium to make one complete Part D premium.

Currently, there are a number of ways that beneficiaries pay either substantially less than the average premiums listed above, or even $0 premiums. First, beneficiaries with incomes below 150 percent of the federal poverty level and limited resources qualify for extra help for premiums and out-of-pocket costs through Part D’s low-income subsidy; beneficiaries with incomes below 100 percent of the federal poverty level and limited resources pay no basic Part D premium and pay $3.90 for branded medicines or $1.30 for generics and biosimilars. Second, over half of all Medicare Advantage enrollees are in $0 premium plans, in which the plan uses a portion of its savings to buy down Medicare Advantage premiums for beneficiaries that enroll in the plan.

Enhanced plans have become increasingly popular with both Part D sponsors and beneficiaries. While enhanced plans have considerable flexibility to offer coverage that meets beneficiaries’ needs, they are constrained in their ability to lower costs in one phase of the benefit—Part D’s coverage gap. Of the over 3.3 million Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in Part D plans who take insulin, 2 million are in a Part D plan and receive a low-income subsidy or are in a Part D plan offered through their employer, which may offer additional coverage in the gap to reduce out-of-pocket costs. Of the remaining 1.3 million beneficiaries, 333,000 (26 percent of the 1.3 million) are in basic plans, while 946,000 (73 percent of the 1.3 million) choose to receive their prescription drug coverage from enhanced plans.

In 2020, as shown in Exhibit 1 above, the coverage gap phase begins when a beneficiary reaches $4,020 in total drug spending, and ends when a beneficiary hits $6,350 in out-of-pocket drug spending. In the coverage gap, beneficiaries are responsible for 25 percent of the negotiated price of each prescription. Manufacturers pay 70 percent of the negotiated price for brand drugs and biologics (including biosimilars) and the insurance plan pays the remaining 5 percent. The quicker a beneficiary moves through the coverage gap, the quicker the manufacturer is relieved of the requirement to pay the manufacturer discounts on applicable drugs, and the quicker the federal government has to pay. In 2018, 75 percent of beneficiaries in Part D who use insulin reached the coverage gap.

Under current Medicare rules, if an enhanced plan offers reduced cost sharing in the coverage gap for an applicable brand or other drug, so the beneficiary pays less than 25 percent of the negotiated price, the manufacturer only discounts 70 percent on the remaining amount, rather than the full 70 percent of the negotiated price. The plan makes up the difference, which increases beneficiary premiums. This financial disincentive for offering supplemental benefits during the coverage gap has resulted in virtually no enhanced plans lowering beneficiary cost sharing in this phase for insulin or other brand drugs, biologics, or biosimilars. Therefore, even in enhanced plans, beneficiaries face fluctuating and unpredictable costs for insulin over the course of the year, as they move from one phase to the next. Given the list prices of insulin, the 25 percent coinsurance required in the coverage gap means patient out-of-pocket costs run anywhere from $70-$180 per thirty-day supply of insulin in this phase, which adds up to on average $675 in annual out-of-pocket costs for insulin.

Studies have shown that patients with chronic diseases who face high cost-sharing requirements have lower medication adherence, which leads to health complications and greater medical costs in the long run. For example, [a meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/17609491?__hstc=201281073.917710d91a5042c74d724fe9a404a014.1774272398233.1774275045897.1775444755888.3&__hssc=201281073.1.1775444755888&__hsfp=e96f7316d8445f45d8b3cd6670f89b9f) found that for patients with congestive heart failure, schizophrenia, lipid disorders, or diabetes, higher cost sharing was associated with increased use of medical services due worse adherence and more frequent discontinuation of therapy. And with respect to Medicare patients with diabetes specifically, [one study](https://www.ajmc.com/journals/issue/2017/2017-vol23-n4/the-relationship-between-adherence-and-total-spending-among-medicare-beneficiaries-with-type-2-diabetes) found that higher prescription drug cost sharing was associated with lower adherence and higher overall medical spending. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) has also found a connection between increased prescription drug adherence and decreased medical costs overall– [CBO estimates](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/43741) that for a 1 percent increase in the number of prescriptions filled by beneficiaries, Medicare’s spending on medical services falls by roughly one-fifth of 1 percent.

## A New Opportunity To Improve Access, Affordability, And Adherence

The Part D Senior Savings Model will test the impact of waiving the current financial disincentive that plans face to offer supplemental benefits in the coverage gap, thereby providing a voluntary opportunity for pharmaceutical manufacturers and enhanced plans to provide insulin at a set copay from the deductible through the coverage gap.

First, CMS will seek applications from pharmaceutical manufacturers that market insulins to participate in the Model. Then, CMS will seek applications from enhanced plans to design Part D formularies that include, at a minimum, a thirty-day supply of at least one of each type of insulin (rapid-acting insulin, regular insulin, intermediate insulin; and long-acting insulin, assuming broad manufacturer participation) at a $35 copay in the deductible, initial coverage, and coverage gap phases of the Part D benefit. Beneficiaries who do not qualify for the low-income subsidy (LIS) currently pay 5 percent of the negotiated price when they reach the catastrophic phase, which should be lower than $35 in most cases. Part D sponsors could offer lower copays than $35 and still maintain all formulary flexibilities and choices.

Beneficiaries who qualify for the low-income subsidy already receive help on their premium and out-of-pocket costs, so they will not be included in this model. Broadly, this Model more closely aligns Part D with how payers, many of whom are also Part D sponsors, are designing plans in the [commercial](https://www.cigna.com/newsroom/news-releases/2019/cigna-and-express-scripts-introduce-patient-assurance-program-to-cap-out-of-pocket-costs-at-25-per-30-day-insulin-prescription) [market](https://cvshealth.com/newsroom/press-releases/cvs-health-rxzero-solution-eliminates-member-out-pocket-costs-diabetes) that offer fixed copays for insulin and other diabetes medications.

Like today, all supplemental benefits in Part D will still come from 1) supplemental premiums paid by beneficiaries or 2) joining an MA plan that offers prescription drug coverage. CMS actuaries predict that the premium increases in Part D enhanced plans will be minimal, on the order of $1 more per month. In exchange for this nominal increase, we anticipate non-LIS beneficiaries in enhanced plans who take insulin will see a 66 percent reduction in average annual out of pocket costs for insulins, from $675 to $229. These are only annual savings from more predictable out-of-pocket cost for their insulin prescriptions and do not factor in both short- and longer-term costs savings from avoidable medical expenses.

By ensuring an affordable and stable cost sharing amount for insulin, we expect the Part D Senior Savings Model to improve insulin compliance and reduce overall medical costs. Our evaluation of the Model will assess whether this is observed. Combined Medicare Advantage-prescription drug plans could reinvest any medical savings in additional benefits for their enrollees. And medical savings associated with participation by standalone Part D plans will be realized by the federal government, since most beneficiaries will be in the government-run fee-for-service Medicare program for their medical benefit.

The federal government is expected to achieve savings on the order of $250 million over five years. This will largely come from reductions in federal reinsurance subsidy spending, as beneficiaries will be moving through the Part D coverage gap more slowly under the Model. Specifically, after the coverage gap phase comes the catastrophic phase, in which the federal government is responsible for 80 percent of costs. Delaying beneficiaries’ arrival at this phase will lead to less federal spending. Because beneficiaries will move through the coverage gap more slowly, CMS projects pharmaceutical manufacturers will pay the coverage gap discount on additional fills, resulting in increased costs for pharmaceutical manufacturers that voluntarily participate in the Model.

Plans may have a substantial number of higher-cost beneficiaries with diabetes enroll if they offer a predictable $35 cost sharing for insulin. CMS’s current risk adjustment processes adjust for this, but to mitigate concerns about potential unanticipated increases in expenses, CMS is offering a temporary narrowing of Part D’s risk corridors for participating plans that see higher-than-average enrollment from insulin-dependent diabetic patients. Also, manufacturers pay substantial rebates (known as direct and indirect remuneration, or DIR) on insulin prescriptions. That means attracting more insulin users would yield a greater amount in manufacturer rebate payments for plans, which may offset premium increases for the plans.

For manufacturers, discount payments for insulin and other Model drugs will be based on the negotiated price prior to any Part D supplemental benefits. As noted above, if beneficiaries do not move as quickly through the coverage gap, manufacturers would pay an additional 70 percent discount payments in the coverage gap. At the same time, participating manufacturers should see increased utilization for their products and plans may have a more adherent population to include when assessing the impact, at a population level, of decreased medical utilization due to use of a drug.

## Broad Impact

Part D Sponsors and insulin manufacturers will be eligible to participate immediately. Part D Sponsors will submit their bids for this benefit design this summer for plans offered during Medicare open enrollment in fall 2020, with coverage in those plans starting in January 2021. CMS intends to clearly identify the plan options that are offering lower-cost insulin in Plan Finder to make it easier for beneficiaries to search for these plans. Plans and manufacturers will have to agree to participate on an annual basis, with the Model ending December 31, 2025.

By testing this Model, CMS aims to allow Part D sponsors and pharmaceutical manufacturers to build a better market for insulin in Part D for beneficiaries, reducing program costs while enhancing the quality of care for beneficiaries. The Model creates more flexibility for plans and manufacturers to better negotiate and to meet beneficiary preferences and needs for lower out-of-pocket costs, bringing to market plan designs and choices with the level of coverage that is most appealing to beneficiaries.

CMS strongly encourages sponsors of enhanced Part D plans and manufacturers of insulin to participate in the Part D Senior Savings Model. The Part D Senior Savings Model could lower beneficiary out-of-pocket costs next year while allowing us to assess the impact of this change on overall medical spending and health outcomes. CMS will monitor and evaluate the impacts of the model, including the costs of insulin outside of the model; based on this data, CMS may consider adding other drugs. By offering a benefit where a 30-day supply of insulin costs no more than $35, plans and manufacturers in the Part D Senior Savings Model will begin to provide much-needed relief at the pharmacy counter for our nation’s seniors with diabetes.
