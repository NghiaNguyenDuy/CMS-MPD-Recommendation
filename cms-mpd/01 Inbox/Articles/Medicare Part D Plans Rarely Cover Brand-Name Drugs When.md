## Abstract

```
Recent press reports and other evidence suggest that Medicare Part D plans may be encouraging
the use of brand-name drugs instead of generics. However, the scope of such practices is unclear.
We examined Medicare Part D formulary coverage and tier placement of matched pairs of brand-
name drugs and generics to quantify how often preferred formulary placement of brand-name
drugs is occurring within and across Part D plans and to assess the cost implications for Medicare
and its beneficiaries. We found that in 2019, 84 percent of 4,176,772 Part D plan-product
combinations had generic-only coverage (that is, the brand-name counterparts were not covered).
Another 15 percent covered both the brand-name and generic versions of a product. For the
small number of products whose brand-name versions were covered preferentially to their generic
equivalents, beneficiary and Medicare prices were generally low for both products. Overall, we
found that most Part D plan formularies are designed to encourage utilization of generics rather
than their brand-name counterparts. Policy makers should continue to monitor Part D formulary
coverage patterns to ensure consistent and generous coverage for generic drugs, given their
important role in reducing prescription drug spending.
```

```
With forty-five million Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in Part D prescription drug plans
and an estimated $98 billion in program spending in 2019, formulary coverage decisions
by Part D plan sponsors can affect overall retail prescription drug spending in the US.^1
Formulary coverage and tier placement—which drugs plans cover and how much patients
pay for them, respectively—influence whether or not a particular drug is affordable and
ultimately accessible to patients.
```
```
When brand-name drugs lose patent-protected status and generic drugs become available,
utilization typically shifts to lower-cost generic drugs (encouraged through formulary
placement and automatic pharmacy substitution laws, among other policies), which has
traditionally generated substantial savings for patients and payers.^2 –^5 However, recent press
reports and other evidence suggest that Part D plans are increasingly listing certain generics
on nonpreferred tiers (those with higher cost sharing),^6 ,^7 listing some generics less favorably
than brand-name drugs,^8 ,^9 or not including certain generics on the formulary at all.^6 ,^7 ,^9
```
```
Stakeholders and scholars have offered numerous reasons for the observed shift of generics
to nonpreferred tiers in Part D, including financial incentives associated with rebate
arrangements, brokered by pharmacy benefit managers, that favor brand-name drugs over
generics.^10 –^13 Generally, rebates are negotiated for brand-name drugs but not for traditional
generics. In Part D, these rebates may be used by plans to offset their total drug costs,
which may help control premiums paid by enrollees but do not lower point-of-sale prices.^14
In cases where a generic price is not discounted enough relative to the brand-name price,
both plans and patients may have financial incentives to use brand-name drugs due to the
manufacturer discounts that are required in the Part D coverage gap.^10
```
```
Previous research has documented two concerning patterns. First, a recent analysis of
generic drug tier placement by Avalere found that generic drugs offered under Medicare
Part D have shifted from lower tier placement (corresponding to lower patient out-of-pocket
spending) to higher tier placement.^6 The study found that by 2019 nearly half of all generic
products were placed in nongeneric tiers, which can increase patient cost sharing. While
this study raised concerns about the placement of generic drugs on Part D formularies, it
did not consider formulary placement for brand-name alternatives or whether plans may be
incentivizing the use of brand-name drugs relative to generics.
```
```
Second, a recent peer reviewed study showed that in 2016, 72 percent of the formularies
for Part D drug-coverage-only plans (that is, stand-alone Part D plans purchased by
beneficiaries in traditional Medicare) placed at least one brand-name product on a lower
tier than its generic equivalent, and among drugs covered by all formularies, 5 percent of
brand-name products were covered on a lower tier.^9 While this study showed that most plans
have at least one brand-name drug on a lower tier than its generic alternative, it did not
examine the frequency with which this occurred across all brand-name drugs for which there
are generics.
```
```
The current study aims to build upon prior work and inform policy discussions related to
Part D coverage by quantifying how often brand-name drugs receive favorable formulary
inclusion or placement relative to generics. Given the overall lower cost of generics relative
```

## Study Data And Methods

```
Data Source and Product Selection
We evaluated Medicare Part D formulary coverage of generic and brand-name drugs in the
period 2012–19 for all Part D stand-alone prescription drug plans and Medicare Advantage
prescription drug plans. We used the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS)
Formulary Reference File from the third quarter of each year in the study period to define
a list of generic drugs for each year. Generic drugs were defined as non-brand-name
therapeutic equivalent products approved through an abbreviated new drug application and
were identified in our data as products without a trade name.^15 In the third quarter of
2018, this file contained 6,275 drug products (including 2,615 generics) that, according to
CMS, may be included on Medicare Part D plan formularies.^16 Using these product lists,
we identified exact brand-name–generic pairs based on the active ingredients, strengths,
and dose form for each drug available in each file.^17 For example, the brand-name drug
Synthroid is available in twelve different doses, and each has a generic equivalent (that is,
an exact match in terms of active ingredients, strength, and dose form) associated with it.
Each pair is considered a unique observation in our primary analysis, meaning that the brand
Synthroid and the corresponding generic levothyroxine accounted for twelve observations.
```
```
This matching process resulted in 1,361 generic drugs with an exact match to a brand-name
drug in 2019. The number of pairs varied by year.
```
```
Study Measures and Analysis
For each product, we used the Prescription Drug Plan Formulary, Pharmacy Network, and
Pricing Information Files for the last quarter of each year^18 to determine median list prices
for drugs whose prescriptions were filled through Part D plans in the prior quarter and the
benefit design for the first quarter of the following calendar year.
```
```
First, we determined the relative frequency of three formulary coverage patterns of the
brand-name–generic pairs in our analysis in each year: covering only the brand-name drug,
only the generic, or both. We refer to these formulary decisions as “plan” decisions, but we
recognize that pharmacy benefit managers are used by plans to make such decisions.
```
```
We constructed a measure based on the product of brand-name–generic pairs and Part
D plans available in a given year to summarize coverage patterns for brand-name and
generic drugs across plans. We refer to this measure as the “plan-product combination,” in
which each observation represents the coverage status of one product for one plan. This
measure allowed us to understand the formulary coverage patterns described above when
we considered all products and plans simultaneously. In 2019 this approach resulted in
4,176,772 plan-product combinations in which plans covered either a specific brand-name
drug, its generic equivalent, or both.
```

```
Second, for plans that included both a brand-name drug and its generic equivalent on
their formularies in 2019, we analyzed formulary placement (whether the brand-name drug
was covered on a lower tier than the generic was or vice versa, or both products were
covered on the same tier) and whether plans employed utilization management restrictions
(prior authorization, step therapy, or quantity limits) on brand-name or generic products to
encourage or discourage utilization of either type of drug when plans covered both types. We
also estimated differences in product point-of-sale prices and patient out-of-pocket spending
under various coverage patterns. Point-of-sale prices in the formulary files represent the
average per fill price at in-area retail pharmacies for a product and plan from the prior
quarter. Patient out-of-pocket spending was estimated using the plan formulary tier and
cost-sharing amounts for the initial Part D coverage phase.
```
```
Finally, we evaluated potential spending impacts on Medicare Part D related to plan
preference for brand-name drugs over generics in cases where such patterns were observed.
For this evaluation, we used data from the CMS Medicare Part D Drug Spending Dashboard
for 2018 to assess utilization and spending on brand-name and generic products with the
same active ingredients.
```
```
Limitations
Our study had some limitations. First, matching drugs by active ingredients, dose, and
formulation may create redundancy by treating all doses and formulations as individual
product pairs for the primary analysis. We completed two alternative analyses that were
restricted to a single dose for each product, selecting the most commonly used dose and the
lowest dose, respectively. In both cases our conclusions regarding patterns of brand-name
and generic drug use mirrored our other findings, so the results are not shown. While prior
research on brand-name–generic competition and market response within a small number
of drug classes typically considered products at the molecule level, and might even have
considered therapeutic substitutes when evaluating plan behavior,^2 ,^3 ,^19 ,^20 the number of
products in our study precluded our use of this approach.
```
```
Second, by requiring brand-name–generic pairs to match on active ingredients, dose,
and formulation, we may have excluded some products whose brand-name counterpart
has exited the market. As an example, the generic product venlafaxine had five dosage
formulations included in the Formulary Reference File, but none was included in our
analysis because venlafaxine’s brand-name counterpart (Effexor) was withdrawn from the
market around the time that an extended release formulation (brand-name Effexor Rx) was
introduced. This behavior is often termed “product hopping.” Approximately half of all
generic products in the Formulary Reference File were excluded for this reason, which
highlights the importance of this phenomenon as an area for future work.
```
```
Third, we considered all product pairs available in a given year, regardless of the time
elapsed since the generic drug’s entry into the market and the number of generic competitors
or therapeutic substitutes—both of which may affect formulary coverage, placement, and
prices. Because of this, year-to-year comparisons in formulary placement decisions should
be viewed cautiously.
```

```
Fourth, we did not directly account for differences in plan enrollment or drug utilization in
our analysis. To partly address this concern, we used Medicare claims from the most recent
year available (2018) to evaluate drug utilization for products when brand-name drugs were
preferred to generics in at least some cases. However, the use of products of interest might
have changed between 2018 and 2019, particularly if generic uptake was delayed.
```
```
Finally, our analysis was limited to generic products with a brand-name drug that matched
the generic in terms of active ingredients, dose, and formulation, so other important issues
such as product hopping and delayed generic drug entry were not explored. However, we
believe that comparing exact brand-name–generic pairs was the optimal approach for our
study, as these products are, by definition, directly substitutable for one another. Thus, we
did not evaluate the appropriateness of tiering or utilization management strategies being
employed by plans for specific products, nor did we evaluate whether there were lower-cost
therapeutic alternatives available when generics were placed on higher tiers than brand-name
drugs were.
```
## Study Results

```
For drugs with both brand-name and generic versions, most Part D plans covered the
generic only (exhibit 1). In only a handful of cases did plans cover the brand-name
drug only. Generic-only coverage has increased over time, with modest decreases in
brand-name-only or both brand-name and generic coverage. Based on our measure of
plan-product combinations, we found that less than 1 percent of our observations (0.
percent) corresponded to coverage of brand-name products only (exhibit 2). Conversely, 84
percent of the observations corresponded to coverage of generics only, while 15 percent
corresponded to coverage of both generic and brand-name versions by the same plan.
```
```
Brand-Name Drugs Covered, Generics Excluded
Of the 1,361 drug products in our analysis, there were 218 products for which at least one
plan covered the brand-name while excluding the generic version in 2019. For more than
half (116) of these products, this coverage pattern was used by 1 percent or less of Part D
plans (online appendix exhibits 2A and 2B).^21 For 5 products, this coverage pattern was
employed by 25 percent or more of the plans.
```
```
Of the 218 products for which the brand-name version only was covered in one or
more plans, most were used by very few Medicare beneficiaries. There were two notable
exceptions. Colcrys, the brand-name version of colchicine (used for the treatment of gout),
had 511,036 Part D claims in 2018, versus 823,633 claims for the generic product. For
Colcrys, average spending per claim was $259, versus $214 for generic colchicine—a
difference of $45. In 2019, generic colchicine was excluded by 20–46 percent of Part D
plans in favor of the brand-name product, depending on the formulation studied (data not
shown).
```
```
Copaxone, the brand-name version of generic glatiramer acetate (used to treat relapsing
multiple sclerosis) had 185,180 Part D claims filed in 2018, versus 27,616 for the generic
product (possibly lower because it was approved only in October 2017). Average spending
```

```
Brand-Name Drugs And Generics Covered
For the 14.8 percent (618,516 of 4,176,772) of product-plan combinations where both
brand-name and generic versions of the same drug product were covered on the same plan
formulary (exhibit 2), we found that brand-name drugs were placed in lower cost-sharing
tiers than their generic equivalents only 1 percent of the time. Generics were placed on a
lower cost-sharing tier than brand-name drugs 40 percent of the time, and brand-name and
generic products were placed on the same tier 59 percent of the time.
```
```
Brand-Name Drugs Placed On Lower Cost-Sharing Tiers Than Generics
There were 125 unique products in which the brand-name drug was placed on a lower
formulary tier than the generic equivalent in at least one plan (9.2 percent of the 1,
products studied) (appendix exhibits 3A and 3B).^21 For most of the 88 products with this
coverage pattern, brand-name drugs were placed on a lower cost-sharing tier than their
generic equivalents in 1 percent or less of plans. In the 1 percent of cases (7,761 of 618,
observations) in which brand-name drugs were placed on a lower tier than their generic
equivalents when both products were covered in the same plan, mean list prices were
$310 for brands and $139 for generics (exhibit 3). However, median list prices were very
low—just over $20—for both brand-name drugs and generics. Under this coverage pattern,
based on list prices and cost-sharing amounts charged for these products, the expected
mean out-of-pocket spending would be $34 for brand-name drugs and $56 for generics. The
higher expected mean out-of-pocket spending for generics reflects plan use of coinsurance
for generics when brand-name drugs are preferred (36 percent of plans that used this
coverage strategy required coinsurance for generics, while only 4 percent required it for the
brand-name equivalents).
```
```
Brand-Name Drugs And Generics On The Same Tier
There were 652 products (47.9 percent of the products in this analysis) in which brand-name
drugs and their generic equivalents were placed on the same tier by at least one plan in 2019.
Of these 652 products, 465 (71.3 percent) were covered on the same tier in 10 percent or less
of the plans (appendix exhibits 4A and 4B).^21
```

```
In the 59 percent (365,160 out of 618,516) of observations in which brand-name drugs and
```

```
When generics and brand-name drugs were both covered on the same tier, beneficiary

products: $1,263 (standard deviation: $2,896) for brand-name drugs versus $638 (SD:

$895) for brand-name drugs, resulting in expected per fill average savings of $194 (SD:
$597) for generic drug use.
```

```
Generics Placed On Lower Cost-Sharing Tiers Than Brand-Name Drugs
There were 1,334 products (98 percent of the products in our analysis) in which generics
were placed on a lower tier than their brand-name equivalents in at least one plan in 2019.
Nearly all of these generic products were placed in lower cost-sharing tiers than brand-name
drugs in at least half of the plans.
```
```
When generics were placed on lower cost-sharing tiers relative to their brand-name
counterparts in the same plan (245,595 of 618,516 observations), mean list prices were
$106 for generics and $406 for brand-name drugs (exhibit 3). Based on total prices and
formulary cost-sharing amounts, the expected average out-of-pocket spending would be $
for generics and $108 for brand-name drugs—a per fill average savings of $91 (SD: $169)
for generic drug use.
```
```
Utilization Management Practices For Brand-Name Drugs And Generics
For brand-name–generic pairs covered in the same plan, we found that quantity limits,
prior authorization, and step therapy were rarely used for either the brand-name or the
generic product in 2019 (exhibit 4). Quantity limits were the most common utilization
management strategy (17 percent of observations), but these access restrictions were nearly
always applied to brand-name and generic products similarly.
```
## Discussion

```
Despite recent reports regarding Part D plan preference for brand-name drugs over generics,
we found no evidence that such behavior was widespread among the plans in 2019. Our
results are consistent with those of prior research in that we observed some cases of
preferential brand-name drug placement on Part D plan formularies.^9 However, when we
examined the frequency with which this occurred across all brand-name drugs for which
there were generics, we found that the opposite behavior was far more common: 84 percent
```

```
of all product-plan combinations covered the generic and excluded the brand-name drug
in 2019, up from 69 percent in 2012. Our findings are also consistent with those of a
recent study showing that Medicare formularies tend to incentivize the use of generics over
brand-name drugs^22 and with prior economics literature showing robust adoption of generic
drugs after patent expiration, particularly for older products.^2 –^5
```
```
Our analysis shows that it was uncommon for brand-name drugs to receive preferred
placement over their generic equivalents—by plans either excluding the generic entirely
or placing the brand-name drug on a lower cost-sharing tier when covering both versions.
These coverage patterns were observed for a small number of products, most of which
were not widely used by Medicare beneficiaries, and typically in a small number of plans.
Rather, our analysis shows that the conventional coverage pattern—generics preferred (that
is, covered on lower cost-sharing tiers) to brand-name drugs—reflects the norm for Part D
plans in 2019 and earlier years.
```
```
While preferential formulary placement of brand-name drugs or exclusion of generic drugs
was not common in our analysis, we found that a small number of brand-name drugs were
subject to higher rates of preferential treatment on Part D plan formularies, despite the
availability of generic equivalents. Although most of these brand-name drugs are not highly
utilized and therefore are not particularly costly to Medicare or its beneficiaries, there are
some exceptions. Better inclusion and coverage of these generic drugs and of all excluded
or nonpreferred generic drugs on Part D formularies could present an opportunity for cost
savings to the Medicare program as well as its beneficiaries.
```
```
We found no evidence of differential use of prior authorization, step therapy, or quantity
limits to influence drug selection when brand-name drugs and generics were both offered
on a formulary. One possible reason for such low use of these utilization management tools
could be the presence of other generic substitution laws that encourage the dispensing of
generics over brand-name drugs unless dispense-as-written is indicated by the prescriber.
Given the large proportion of plans that elect to cover only a generic drug on their formulary,
this may further reduce the need for utilization management of such products.
```
```
Finally, for the subset of observations in which generics and brand-name drugs were placed
on the same tier, it is possible that plans are indifferent in their coverage strategy for brands
and their generic equivalents if both have low or similar list prices, or if the plans are unable
to negotiate rebates on the brand-name drugs that would shift the financial incentive to
giving them preferred placement, in relation to their generic equivalents. While beyond the
scope of our analysis, this behavior merits further exploration since it has cost implications
for both plans and patients. Patients who used a brand-name drug instead of a generic when
both products are covered on the same tier could face unnecessary financial burden when
paying deductibles or coinsurance (versus flat copayments) if the underlying list price of the
brand-name drug was higher than that of the generic.
```

## Conclusion

```
Overall, our analysis suggests that the vast majority of Part D plan formularies are designed
to encourage utilization of generic drugs rather than their brand-name counterparts, with
most plans covering only generic versions of drug products or placing generics on lower
cost-sharing tiers than brand-name drugs. Given that preferential tiering of brand-name
products and the exclusion of generics is not widespread in Part D plans, this suggests
that policy making designed to address this relatively infrequent coverage pattern may be
unlikely to yield significant savings.
```
```
Policy makers should continue to monitor Part D formulary coverage patterns to ensure
consistent and generous coverage for generic drugs, given their important role in reducing
prescription drug spending.
```
