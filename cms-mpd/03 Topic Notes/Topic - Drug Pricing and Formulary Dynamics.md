# Definition

Drug pricing and formulary dynamics are the set of forces that turn list prices, negotiated prices, tier placement, and plan design into the beneficiary's final out-of-pocket cost.

## Core Details

- List price and beneficiary out-of-pocket cost are not the same thing.
- Generic drugs can still be poor value to the beneficiary if plan pricing and tiering are distorted.
- At the same time, the imported Health Affairs generics study suggests most Part D formularies still generally steer use toward generics, so the important question is when exceptions arise and how much they cost.
- Utilization restrictions and exclusions increased materially over 2011 to 2020, especially for brand-name-only and higher-cost compounds.
- Stand-alone PDPs can be more restrictive than MA-PD plans, which suggests different financial incentives around drug spending versus total medical spending.
- Protected-class policy can improve access but can also weaken plan leverage against price growth.
- Formulary placement, coinsurance, and non-coverage can matter as much as the underlying drug price.
- Exclusion from the formulary is a more extreme restriction than prior authorization or step therapy because it can remove covered access altogether absent appeal or full out-of-pocket payment.
- Integrated MA-PD plans and stand-alone PDPs can have different formulary incentives because integrated plans may capture downstream medical savings while stand-alone drug plans cannot.
- Risk adjustment does not remove all strategic behavior in a stand-alone drug benefit, because plans can still target specific conditions through drug-level coverage rules.
- A drug can still be labeled preferred while exposing the beneficiary to co-insurance rather than a flat co-payment, which can transmit list-price growth directly into out-of-pocket spending.
- Formulary inclusion by itself does not guarantee easy access, because tier placement, prior authorization, and step therapy can still materially change beneficiary use.
- Biosimilar savings pressure is a good example: a plan may cover a biosimilar but still fail to encourage substitution if it gives the reference product the same tier placement and utilization-management treatment.
- GLP-1s are another live example: even with broad diabetes-related coverage, plans can tighten access through near-universal prior authorization and higher coinsurance when spending pressure rises.
- OIG's 2025 diabetes-drug brief shows that a small set of high-spend drugs can grow much faster than overall Part D enrollment, which helps explain why formulary policy, negotiation, and fiscal oversight increasingly overlap.
- MedPAC's DIR presentation shows that rebate levels vary sharply across drug classes and organizational structures, which helps explain why net prices, list prices, and beneficiary-facing prices can move differently.
- Pricing files and benchmarking tools such as NADAC can expose large variation across plans for the same product.

## Why It Matters

Without this concept, it is easy to misread plan materials and think that brand versus generic, covered versus uncovered, or list price versus cost sharing are simple relationships. They are not.

## Examples

- A brand-name drug can cost the beneficiary less than a generic alternative because of formulary placement and negotiated pricing.
- Two plans can cover the same drug but place it on different tiers, producing materially different annual out-of-pocket costs.
- Two plans can cover the same therapeutic area but differ because one excludes a compound entirely while another allows access with prior authorization or step therapy.
