# Summary

These materials show how insulin costs in Part D became a major affordability problem, how formulary tiering and benefit phases shaped beneficiary costs, and how policy responses moved from a limited model to broader cost protections.

## Key Points

- KFF reports that aggregate out-of-pocket insulin spending in Part D quadrupled from 2007 to 2017, from $236 million to $984 million.
- For insulin users without LIS, average annual out-of-pocket spending rose from $324 in 2007 to $580 in 2017.
- The newer KFF update extends that trend through 2020, showing aggregate insulin out-of-pocket spending rose from $236 million in 2007 to $1.03 billion in 2020 while the number of Part D insulin users doubled from 1.6 million to 3.3 million.
- Before the IRA-era protections, insulin users often paid much more in the coverage gap because a flat copay in initial coverage could turn into 25 percent coinsurance.
- The 2020 Senior Savings Model was a targeted attempt to offer a predictable $35 monthly insulin copay in participating enhanced plans.
- KFF's 35-dollar cap explainer clarifies the policy sequence: the Senior Savings Model applied only to participating plans from 2021 through 2023, while the Inflation Reduction Act required all Part D plans to cap covered insulin at $35 per month starting January 1, 2023 and removed deductibles for Part D insulin.
- Imported reform sources indicate that insulin later became subject to a broader $35 monthly cap across Part D plans, reducing the need to rely on plan-specific model participation.
- The new 2025 beneficiary-facing article is lower-evidence than the policy briefs and research papers, but it is useful as an example of how current insulin protections are being explained to the public.

## Why It Matters

Insulin is a strong case study for how Part D design, formulary placement, and list-price trends affect real patient affordability. It is also a good bridge between beneficiary-facing policy and plan-level pricing strategy.

## Useful Evidence

- The newer KFF update shows insulin use reached 3.3 million Part D beneficiaries in 2020 and that aggregate out-of-pocket spending exceeded $1 billion before broader protections took effect.
- The Health Affairs piece says about 3.3 million Medicare beneficiaries relied on one or more common insulin forms and that 75 percent of insulin users in Part D reached the coverage gap in 2018.
- KFF shows that the same insulin product can generate very different beneficiary costs depending on tier placement, coinsurance rules, and whether the beneficiary reaches the coverage gap.
- The KFF cap explainer is especially useful because it distinguishes the limited model-participation period from the universal statutory cap that started in 2023.
- The imported PDF title suggests an additional research angle on formulary strategy under changing insulin policy.
- The beneficiary-facing article usefully reflects current public messaging that the $35 monthly cap now matters more than the older model-participation logic.
