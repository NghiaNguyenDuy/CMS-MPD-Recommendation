# Summary

These sources explain why list prices, AWP-based contracting, formulary tiering, and protected-class rules can create pricing behavior that is hard for beneficiaries to interpret and can produce misleading out-of-pocket signals.

## Key Points

- The 46brooklyn article argues that AWP is a poor benchmark for generic drug pricing and shows large plan-level price variation for the same drug.
- The ski slope framing is useful for understanding how two beneficiaries can face very different plan costs for the same medication.
- The Health Affairs utilization restrictions study reports that the share of non-protected-class compounds facing formulary exclusion or prior authorization or step therapy rose from 31.9 percent in 2011 to 44.4 percent in 2020.
- By 2020, Part D plans excluded an average of 44.7 percent of brand-name-only compounds, and 68.4 percent of brand-name-only compounds faced some form of restriction.
- The same study finds restrictions were more common for brand-name-only compounds, for more expensive compounds, and in stand-alone PDPs than in MA-PD plans.
- NBER work on the original Part D design argues that risk adjustment in a stand-alone drug benefit can itself create perverse incentives for formulary design and pharmaceutical pricing, so oversight has to focus on plan incentives as well as consumer protection.
- The same NBER paper suggests that a uniform-premium rule would reduce diversity in quality but could reduce budget uncertainty and upward pressure on prices.
- Another NBER paper finds that integrated MA-PD plans and stand-alone PDPs design formularies differently because integrated plans can capture medical-cost offsets under Parts A and B and can also target more profitable beneficiaries.
- A third NBER paper models Part D supply-side regulation and finds that insurers price near marginal cost, consumers buy too few and too socially costly PDP plans, and subsidy design plus LIS rules materially affect welfare.
- The open-access brand-versus-generic coverage study adds a clarifying counterweight: by 2019, 84 percent of matched plan-product combinations had generic-only coverage, 15 percent covered both the brand and the generic, and brand drugs were placed on a lower tier only about 1 percent of the time when both were covered.
- A newer open-access cost-sharing study shows that PDPs increasingly used co-insurance rather than flat co-payments for preferred branded drugs from 2020 to 2024, while MA-PDs mostly kept co-payments, which meant PDP beneficiary exposure rose more directly with list prices.
- The negotiation-program coverage study shows that average plan coverage for the first 10 eventually selected drugs rose from 82 percent in 2019 to 91.3 percent in 2023, but also warns that access can still be shaped by utilization management and tier placement even when formulary inclusion is high.
- The JAMA study reports that 88.1 percent of brand-name protected-class drugs had list price increases above inflation over the 2016 to 2017 period, and 93.8 percent exceeded inflation over 2012 to 2017 among drugs with five-year data.
- The same study notes that median exclusions of protected-class drugs were 0 percent in Part D, versus higher exclusions in commercial and ACA marketplace plans.
- The Health Affairs generics paper adds an important counterweight: in 2019, 84 percent of matched plan-product combinations had generic-only coverage, 15 percent covered both brand and generic, and brand-name drugs were placed on a lower tier than generics only about 1 percent of the time when both were covered.
- The imported PDF title suggests another beneficiary-facing distortion: some brand-name drugs can cost beneficiaries less than generics because of formulary and cost-sharing design.

## Why It Matters

This theme explains why "cheaper drug" does not automatically mean "lower beneficiary cost" and why plan review must focus on formulary placement, negotiated pricing, and cost-sharing structure rather than broad assumptions about brands and generics.

## Useful Evidence

- The ski slope article also points to CMS pricing files and NADAC comparisons as a data path for plan-level analysis.
- The utilization restrictions paper shows that formulary exclusion is not just a pricing detail but a growing access-management strategy that can materially change beneficiary choice sets.
- The NBER insurer-incentive papers help explain why plan design varies not only because of bargaining or cost control, but also because integrated and fragmented insurers face different selection and cross-service spending incentives.
- The supply-side welfare paper is useful because it ties bid rules, subsidies, and LIS eligibility to plan behavior rather than treating premiums and formularies as separate topics.
- The open-access preferred-brand cost-sharing study is useful because it shows how rebate-driven co-insurance design can raise beneficiary spending even when the plan labels a drug as preferred.
- The selected-drug coverage study is useful because it separates broad formulary inclusion from the narrower question of actual access under tiering and utilization management.
- The JAMA article frames protected-class policy as a tradeoff between plan leverage and beneficiary access.
- The Health Affairs paper suggests that outright brand preference over generics exists, but is relatively uncommon in the matched pairs they studied, which means the main story is not "brands usually beat generics" but rather that specific exceptions and tiering rules can still matter a lot.
- This theme links directly to insulin pricing, specialty drug affordability, and annual plan comparison.
