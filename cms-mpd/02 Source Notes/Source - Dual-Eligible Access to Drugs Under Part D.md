# Summary

These sources focus on one of Part D's most vulnerable populations: dual-eligible beneficiaries enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid. They show that formulary coverage for commonly used drugs has generally remained high, while also highlighting why transition policy and counseling still matter for access.

## Raw Imports

- [[01 Inbox/Web Imports/Part D Plans Generally Include Drugs Commonly Used by Dual-Eligible Enrollees 2025|OIG 2025 dual-eligible access report]]
- [[01 Inbox/Web Imports/Trends in Dual-Eligible Enrollees Access to Drugs Under Part D 2011-2025|OIG trend snapshot for 2011-2025]]
- [[01 Inbox/Web Imports/Effects of Transitioning to Medicare Part D on Access to Drugs for Medical Conditions among Dual Enrollees with Cancer|Open-access study on dual enrollees with cancer]]

## Key Points

- OIG's 2025 annual review found that the 378 unique formularies used by 5,178 Part D plans covered almost all of the drugs commonly used by dual-eligible enrollees, with only a small number of commonly used drugs not covered by most formularies.
- OIG therefore concluded that dual-eligible enrollees can generally expect access to most commonly used drugs in 2025 regardless of plan enrollment.
- The longer OIG trend snapshot shows this was not a one-year anomaly. Coverage of drugs commonly used by dual-eligible enrollees has been consistently high since 2011, and the number of commonly used drugs covered by all formularies has increased over time.
- These OIG findings are reassuring at the broad formulary level, but they do not eliminate operational issues around plan assignment, transitions, utilization management, or beneficiary understanding.
- The open-access cancer study adds historical transition evidence from the 2006 shift into Part D. It suggests that removing Medicaid drug caps under Part D modestly improved access to some noncancer medications for dual enrollees with cancer.
- That same study found increases in lipid-lowering medication use and some narrowing of white-Black gaps in medication use after the transition, which suggests Part D's floor of coverage could matter for both access and disparities.

## Why It Matters

Dual-eligible beneficiaries are high-need, low-income enrollees who rely heavily on stable drug access. Broad formulary inclusion is necessary, but these sources show that access also depends on transition rules, assignment processes, and whether beneficiaries or counselors can navigate plan differences.

## Useful Evidence

- The annual OIG reports are useful because they focus on the exact statutory monitoring task Congress required for dual-eligible access.
- The 2011-2025 trend view is useful because it shows stability over time rather than only a single cross-section.
- The cancer-transition study is useful because it provides patient-level evidence that changing the underlying coverage rules can affect access and disparities, not just abstract formulary percentages.
