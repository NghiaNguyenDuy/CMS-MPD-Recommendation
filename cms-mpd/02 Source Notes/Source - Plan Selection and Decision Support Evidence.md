# Summary

These articles and PDFs show that Medicare beneficiaries often struggle to choose cost-effective Part D plans, benefit from guided decision support, and still face usability problems with Plan Finder and plan details even after the tool narrows the option set.

## Key Points

- The PCORI CHOICE project combined focus groups, ethnographic observation of Plan Finder use, a comparative usability study, and a randomized controlled trial to test whether a patient-centered online tool could improve Part D plan choice.
- In the CHOICE randomized trial, 36 percent of beneficiaries using the version with expert recommendations switched plans, compared with 31 percent using the version without expert recommendations and 28 percent in the control arm; the expert-recommendation arm also improved satisfaction with the choice process.
- The CHOICE report found larger estimated spending reductions in the treatment arms, but those differences were not statistically significant, and it found no clear improvement in decisional conflict.
- The CHOICE1.0 usability study reports that participants were more satisfied with the patient-centered choice tool than with Medicare.gov for choosing a plan, understanding information, and ease of use.
- The Auburn University and Alabama SHIP partnership paper reports that more than 80 pharmacy students each year assisted more than 120 beneficiaries and generated projected beneficiary savings that exceeded $800 in multiple years.
- The national qualitative survey found recurring frustration with high costs, plan restrictions, and the need for selection assistance.
- The administrative-data paper reports that fewer than 25 percent of beneficiaries enrolled in plans that were ex ante as good as the least-cost plan and estimates roughly $300 per year in excess expected spending on average.
- The Heiss administrative-data paper and the NBER Digest summary both reinforce that fewer than 10 percent of individuals enrolled in what would have been their most cost-effective plans and that average losses were roughly $300 per year.
- The Medicare Rights Plan Finder report says the tool narrowed options effectively but often left beneficiaries unable to decipher plan details, restrictions, and likely annual out-of-pocket exposure.
- The Avalere interviews say Plan Finder remains the central comparison tool for beneficiaries, caregivers, and SHIP staff, but it should better show monthly costs under redesign, smoothing payments, formulary coverage and tier placement, year-over-year tier changes, saved results, and language-access support.

## Why It Matters

These findings explain why a Medicare study vault needs both policy notes and workflow notes. Beneficiaries do not simply need more information; they need information presented in a way that supports decisions.

## Useful Evidence

- The PCORI CHOICE report gives the strongest end-to-end user-centered evidence in the vault because it documents both tool design and experimental evaluation rather than only observational frustration with plan choice.
- The CHOICE1.0 paper links better user experience to plan choices that were more consistent with expert recommendations.
- The Auburn paper shows that community-based counseling infrastructure can produce measurable savings while also training future pharmacists.
- The administrative-data paper gives a stronger quantitative anchor for plan-choice inefficiency than the shorter NBER digest summary alone.
- The direct NBER working paper version is useful because it explicitly compares beneficiary outcomes with a static decision rule similar to Medicare's Plan Finder and still finds average losses of about $300 per year.
- The Avalere and Medicare Rights recommendations converge on the same design themes: clearer annual cost presentation, simpler detail pages, easier comparison retention, and stronger alignment between Plan Finder and beneficiary support workflows.

## Questions
